      02 · FOLLOW THE EVIDENCE

      Which names need a different view?

      Two hulls: which name?

      The Viti chapter describes two kata. The supplied terminology uses kata for both hulls of a drua. A museum photograph uses kata and cama for its unequal hulls. What vessel, speaker and complete phrase would help us compare these uses?

      Over or through?

      Hazlewood says yarabale passes over the domodomo, not through its hole. Domodali describes fastening or passing a rope there. The Uto ni Yalo Trust calls a halyard viribale. Can a sailor show which line goes through on a particular rig?

      What needs a real model?

      The house, raised shelf, mast support and steering oar now have their own views. Which smaller joins—kola, taka, masi, drega—would still need a close-up of construction? What would you ask a builder to show?

      HERITAGE · SCIENCE · DESIGN · TOMORROW

      What could this drua set in motion?

      Imagine a young Fijian designer beside this drua, asking a builder to trace the karikaritu down to the tau. Each name opens a design question: how the sail is held, how the two kata carry a load, and how a damaged part might be repaired. The next sketch could serve a short coastal journey, a fishing trip, or a boat carrying supplies where fuel is costly. Which idea is worth exploring?

      People are already asking related questions. A Fiji National University proposal explores a sailing outrigger with a small backup motor and possible solar or wind assistance. A Pacific maritime presentation describes voyaging craft that bring traditional lashings together with modern navigation and solar-electric propulsion. These are other vessels and design efforts. They give us examples to question, test, and learn from as we study this drua.

      The Uto ni Yalo Trust offers a living example in Fiji: its wind- and solar-powered canoe helps people practise voyaging, navigation and ocean care today. What might begin in your community if an experienced sailor taught one young person to read the wind—and that learner returned to teach another?

      Names, measurements, experiments, and stories could all change the sketch. A learner might compare the hulls, test a small sail in different winds, ask a sailor where a rope leads, or ask a community which knowledge may be taught or adapted. Then comes the harder question: would a new boat actually be safe, useful, affordable to repair, and respectful of the people whose knowledge helped shape it?

      One beginning is close at hand: choose a ring that might be wrong. Ask a knowledgeable person to show what they see, record their account with permission, and let that correction change the next drawing.

      02 · TEST THE LOAD

      Could two hulls serve a new journey?

      Look at the two , then follow the beneath the . Where might the weight travel?

      03 · KEEP THE RELATIONSHIPS

      Who will carry the skills into the next voyage?

      Compare the and the . A name can open a conversation about a place, its use, and a skill someone can demonstrate.

      Build the tools: locate the house and the raised shelf, then use their names in a question to a knowledgeable person. What can the model show, and what can only a person explain?

      1. This inquiry uses two kata for the drua, while another source distinguishes kata and cama. Which canoe, community and complete Fijian phrase would you compare before deciding whether the accounts differ?
      2. Who could still show a learner how to rig, steer, read local winds or repair a join? How might several short lessons and a shared task help the learner practise the words and the movements with that person's permission?
      3. If new equipment changed the house or shelf, whose work or practices might change too? Could local builders repair the proposal after rough weather? What would their answers make you keep or change before inviting another young person aboard?

      Bring it together: with permission, plan one short lesson that pairs a Fijian part name with a real action, such as rigging, steering or repairing. What could the learner teach next, and what must remain in the knowledge holder's care?
